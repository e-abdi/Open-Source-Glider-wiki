Deployment and Recovery
++++++++++++++++++++++++

This is usually the single most expensive part of a glider operation, simply because it typically requires the use of a boat or other large vessel.

Small boats with easy access to the water are preferred for both deployment and recovery, even if they need to be launched from a larger vessel. But of course, this is not always possible, and sometimes a glider has to be deployed directly from a ship in the open sea.

.. image:: /images/seaglider-deployment-high-seas.png

Procedure
=========

The procedure is fairly similar for both Seaglider and Slocum. It usually involves sliding the glider into the water using its corresponding cradle, with a safety line attached until the glider's buoyancy is confirmed.

Slocum usually slides in with some speed, so most of the air is removed automatically as it enters the water. For Seaglider, I usually prefer to hold it by the antenna mast once it is in the water and push it in to remove any remaining air bubbles, which gives a more reliable surface position afterwards.

For a more detailed procedure, see `Glider Operation Best Practices <https://e-abdi.github.io/Glider-Operation-Best-Practices/deployment/>`_.

SeaExplorer has a nice cradle which makes recovery easier.

Thoughts for Tuba
=====================

If we create a glider that is robust and very easy to deploy and recover, we can rely on efforts like `Sail for Science <https://sailforscience.com/>`_ and `FVON <https://www.fvon.org/>`_ to use existing vessels already at sea to carry out science, rather than requiring a dedicated research vessel for every deployment.

There are also many efforts and incentives, such as the `Poseidon Principles <https://www.poseidonprinciples.org/finance/#home>`_, for shipowners to make their shipping greener, and helping collect ocean data could be part of that. We could even imagine incentives such as tax deductions or better insurance deals for shipping companies that spend a percentage of their time supporting science.
